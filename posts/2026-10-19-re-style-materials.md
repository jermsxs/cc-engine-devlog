I started a new track on the 19th and called it Track M, for materials. Until then the house's walls and floors were textures generated by a script. They were readable and a little flat. Resident Evil 7 and Village build their surfaces differently. Each surface starts as a photograph of a real material, and grime is layered on top in the places where water, hands and feet go. This post covers the first four steps of that track: how a colour texture is compressed, how a library material is described, how scanned materials replaced the generated ones, and how damp and dirt are laid over them. Streaming, scanned decals and a comparison against my reference are in the next post.

## BC7 with two colours per block

Photo scans are full of small sharp detail: the edge of a stain, a crack, the dark gap between two floorboards, tile grout. Before I brought scans in, I wanted the texture compression to handle edges.

### How block compression works

> **Background: block compression and BC7.** A GPU cannot afford to keep every texture as raw pixels, so textures are stored in a block-compressed format that the texture units decode directly while sampling. BC7 divides the image into 4 by 4 blocks and stores each block in 128 bits, one byte per pixel, a 4:1 saving over 8-bit RGBA. A block holds a small recipe: one or more pairs of endpoint colours, and for each of the 16 pixels an index that says how far along the line between its pair it sits. Decoding is an interpolation between the endpoints with a fixed weight table, for example 4 levels, 8 levels or 16 levels depending on the index width. BC7 has eight modes that trade these quantities against each other. Some modes split the block into two or three subsets, using one of 64 fixed partition patterns, and each subset then has its own endpoint pair. More subsets mean that each pair describes a simpler region, but each endpoint is stored with fewer bits and each index with fewer levels.

My encoder originally wrote one mode, mode 6. It has a single subset over the whole block, RGBA endpoints of 7 bits plus a low bit each, and 16 levels between them. That suits smooth gradients such as plaster, wood and cloth, and it does not band. It cannot represent two unrelated colours in one block. All 16 pixels have to lie near one straight line through colour space, so a block that holds light plaster beside a dark crack is smeared along that line.

![A 4 by 4 BC7 block with one subset and with two subsets](/images/2026-10-19-re-style-materials/bc7-block.svg "Mode 6 describes the block with one pair of endpoint colours. Modes 1 and 3 split the pixels into two subsets and give each its own pair.")

### What the encoder does now

I added modes 1 and 3, both for opaque blocks. They split the block with one of the 64 partitions. Mode 1 uses 6-bit endpoints with a low bit shared per subset and 8 levels per subset. Mode 3 uses 7-bit endpoints with a low bit each and 4 levels, so it trades levels for more precise endpoints. For each block the encoder does the following.

1. It ranks all 64 partitions with a quick estimate. For each one it fits a principal axis to each subset and sums the scatter the two lines leave.
2. It fully encodes the best three partitions in both modes.
3. It keeps whichever of those candidates and mode 6 has the lowest error against the original block.

Trying all 64 partitions instead of three improves quality by only 0.1 to 0.2 dB and takes four times as long to encode.

To trust the encoder I wrote `Bc7GpuDecodeTests`. It builds 4,096 random blocks in modes 1, 3 and 6 and decodes each one two ways: with the engine's own decoder, and on Windows' software GPU (WARP), which follows the BC7 specification exactly. The results match bit for bit. To check that the test can fail, I broke one entry of my partition table on purpose, and 22 blocks differed. Round trips behave as expected: flat colours stay within 1 per channel, and blocks made of two unrelated colour pairs stay within 4, with at least 600 of 640 using a two-colour mode.

The gain is on textures with edges. Measured on 512 by 512 crops of the demo's own textures:

| Texture | Before (mode 6 only) | After | Blocks using mode 1 or 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woven fabric scan | 45.91 dB | 47.05 dB | 67% |
| Wallpaper | 55.50 dB | 56.40 dB | 54% |
| Wall tile | 53.68 dB | 53.92 dB | 73% |
| Herringbone parquet | 52.21 dB | 52.34 dB | 12% |
| Wood scan | 54.52 dB | 54.57 dB | 14% |
| Smooth plaster scan | 51.41 dB | 51.42 dB | 2% |

Smooth plaster gains nothing because mode 6 already suited it. GPU time on the house bench is unchanged, within -0.4% to +0.8% per pose, and the images change by 0.01 to 0.09 per pixel on average out of 255, up to 27 on the bathroom tiles. Raising the cache version made every texture encode again once, so the house started in 44 seconds instead of 12. The next start took 12 seconds again.

![Bathroom tile grout at four times zoom, before and after](/images/2026-10-19-re-style-materials/bathroom-tiles-before-after.jpg "Tile grout at 4x zoom, before (left) and after (right). The grout lines are slightly cleaner. At normal size the difference is hard to see.")

This is hard to see at normal size, and I expect it to matter more with scanned materials.

## Wear, grime and sheen in library materials

The house's walls, floors and trim use a material library, one file that lists each surface's textures and numbers. Imported glTF models could already carry three extra surface effects: worn edges where paint chips to the wood, grime that gathers in crevices and near the floor, and cloth sheen. Library materials could not set any of them, and the renderer used fixed defaults. Each library material can now set them, along with how strongly its ambient occlusion map darkens. They are available in the file, in a new "Wear, grime and sheen" section of the Materials panel, and through `material_set` over MCP. A value is written to the file only when it differs from the default.

The check showed a limit. Wear and grime are driven by masks baked into a mesh's vertices, which record where its edges and crevices are. The walls, floors and ceilings are built-in boxes, and their masks are all zero. On library materials these two effects therefore matter only when a material dresses an imported mesh. Grime on walls and floors needed a different mask, which is the layering described below. I left out metallic maps because no library surface in the house is metal, and 16-bit height maps because the deepest parallax is 4 mm and an 8-bit step is 0.016 mm, far too small to see.

## The house on photo scans

### How a scanned material is made

> **Background: photogrammetry, scanned PBR materials and de-lighting.** A scan is made by photographing a real surface, either from many positions, where software matches features between photographs and reconstructs fine geometry (photogrammetry), or under several known light directions, where the change in shading from one image to the next gives the surface normals (photometric stereo). The software turns the result into maps: albedo (base colour), a normal map for the fine relief, roughness, a height map and sometimes ambient occlusion. Albedo should contain only the colour of the material. Shadows and highlights from the capture must be removed, which is called de-lighting, because the engine will light the surface itself and baked-in lighting would be counted twice. A scan also has a known size in metres. That matters, because plaster marks should appear at plaster scale and not stretched across a wall.

I use free CC0 scans from Poly Haven, brought in with a new `ingest.py polyhaven-texture` command. It keeps only the maps the engine reads, checks them, and writes a licence sidecar that records the scan's real size. Six scans now dress the house.

| Surface | Scan | Real size | Library material |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground-floor walls | white plaster | 1 m | `scan.wall.plaster` |
| Upstairs walls | grey plaster | 1 m | `scan.wall.grey_plaster` |
| Ceilings | interior ceiling | 2 m | `scan.ceiling.plaster` |
| Study, kitchen, storage, upstairs, stairs | old wood floor, separate planks | 3 m | `scan.floor.old_planks` |
| Corridor, foyer, parlor, dining | herringbone parquet | 3.4 m | `scan.floor.herringbone` |
| Parlor, dining, master, nursery | decrepit wallpaper under the damask | 2.5 m | `scan.wall.aged_wallpaper` |

Each surface repeats at the scan's real size, and the 1 m plasters are tiled at 2 m so that the repeat is harder to spot. That gives walls about 1,000 pixels per metre and floors 600 to 680. I set each material's colour factor so that its rooms are as bright on average as before, so that only the detail changed and not the exposure.

Two decisions were specific to my preferences. The parquet scan's own roughness map describes varnished parquet and averages 0.10, which is close to a mirror. I left that map out and gave the floor a flat 0.92, because I do not want shiny floors. And none of the CC0 wallpapers carries the damask pattern the house uses, so I print the existing damask over the decrepit-wallpaper scan. The scan's stains, tears, streaks and paper relief age the print, in place of the ageing I generated before.

![Close-ups of the corridor floor, foyer floor, parlor wallpaper and an upstairs wall](/images/2026-10-19-re-style-materials/close-ups.jpg "Scanned surfaces up close. Top: parquet with real board edges. Bottom left: aged wallpaper. Bottom right: plaster with real cracks and trowel relief.")

![Six house views after the move to scans](/images/2026-10-19-re-style-materials/house-after.jpg "Six house views on scanned materials.")

A test failed on the way and it was useful. `RayTracerTests` checks that the committed bakes are current, and the hallway's were not, because the bake key covered the whole material library, so adding six materials marked them stale. I rebaked both scenes, with the hallway taking 16 seconds. Measured against the version before the scans, GPU time rose by 1.0 to 4.2% per view, about 0.03 ms. Texture memory went from 143 to 226 MB, video memory from 476 to 559 MB, and the number of material textures from 81 to 94.

## Damp, stains and dirt as material layers

Real plaster is rarely clean. A damp layer creeps up from the floor, a stain hangs from the ceiling, and floors hold dirt in the grooves. A tiling base material cannot show any of that by itself.

### Masks and height blending

> **Background: material layering and height blending.** A layered material combines a base material with one or more overlay materials, each with its own colour, roughness and normal map, according to a mask. The mask can come from vertex colours, from a texture, from the geometry (height above the floor), or from noise. If the mask is used directly as a blend weight, the overlay fades in as a smooth gradient and looks printed on. Dirt behaves differently. It collects in low places first, so the boundary of a damp patch follows the relief of the surface. Height blending reproduces that. The mask is compared with the base material's height map, so that where the base is low the overlay appears at a lower mask value, and where the base is high it appears later. The edge of the layer then follows the bumps instead of a smooth curve.

In the shader a layer's coverage is computed in three parts. The first is a shape value from the layer's mask type. For a damp band it is `saturate(1 - 0.5 * distance / band)`, where `distance` is the height above the storey's floor, so the shape is full at the floor and fades to zero at twice the band. World-space noise, two octaves weighted 0.65 and 0.35, breaks up the border, and because the noise is in world space it is continuous from one wall piece to the next. The second part is the height blend, which shifts the threshold by the base's height: `saturate((shape * amount - 0.5 - (height - 0.5) * 0.5) * contrast + 0.5)`. A low height gives a lower threshold, so pits fill first. The third part, a factor `saturate(shape * amount * 4)`, makes the height blend able to move a border but never start one. Without it, deep pits far from any mask picked up dirt.

### Storeys

A plain box does not know where its room's floor is. Walls run down into the floor slab, and the foyer's wall runs through both storeys. The scene's render settings now hold each storey's floor and ceiling heights. A box's own bottom and top count only when they lie within 0.45 m of a storey's floor or ceiling. A lintel over a door gets no rising damp, and a two-storey wall has no stripe halfway up.

### What I built

A library material can lay up to two other library materials over itself. Each layer says where it gathers: a band rising from the floor, a band hanging from the ceiling, along the borders of a floor or ceiling box, or in patches of noise anywhere. All of it lives in `materials.json`, the Materials panel and `material_set`. In the house, plaster and wallpaper get damp 25 to 30 cm up from the floor and a tea-coloured tide mark under the ceiling. Ceilings are stained along their edges and in blotches. Floors get dark dirt patches that settle into the grooves of the boards. Everything stays matte at 0.95 roughness or higher. The layer materials come from two more CC0 scans, concrete_wall_006 and brushed_concrete_2.

My first attempt was too loud. A cracked-plaster scan on the ceilings drew a dark band like reptile skin around every room. Floor dirt covered whole stair risers. The height blend let deep pits in the middle of a floor pick up dirt where no mask existed. I changed the stain to the damp scan, tinted and nearly flat, dropped the cracked scan, restricted "edges" to the border of a box's top or bottom face and skipped axes shorter than four bands, and made the height blend unable to start a border.

## Results and open items

On a ten-view bench at 1600 by 900, the layers cost 0.07 to 0.12 ms of GPU time per view, inside the 0.3 ms I had budgeted, and the mean image change is 0 to 2.3 out of 255. Any edit to the material library makes every scene's bakes stale, so I rebaked both scenes. The 100 tests pass.

Open items:

- Floor grime along the walls does not show, because the floor boxes run under the walls and hide their own edges. The dirt uses patches for now. A real fix needs the walls' footprint or masks driven by ambient occlusion.
- The depth prepass, screen-space reflections and GTAO see the base material and not the layers. That does not matter while everything is matte.
- The game reports the house's bakes as stale at startup even directly after a bake, while the headless editor reports them current. It did the same before this work, so I suspect the game's scene view adds entities the editor's does not.
- Trim, tiles and fabric were still the older surfaces, and the hallway scene was still on the old materials. Streaming and scanned decals followed.
