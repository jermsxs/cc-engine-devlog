After the lighting rewrite, the hallway had exactly one shadow. It came from a single projected map, aimed by hand from a spot near one lamp at a hand-picked target, and the flashlight cast no shadow at all. That is the kind of thing I wanted to get rid of. Light without shadow looks pasted on, and a horror game lives on the dark shape behind a door frame.

So the goal for the day: every shadow comes from a real light, in the position of that light, and I don't hand-aim anything.

## How a shadow map works

> **Concept: Shadow maps.** Imagine standing where the light is and taking a picture, but instead of colours, every pixel records *how far away* the nearest surface is. That depth picture is a *shadow map*. Later, when the camera draws a wall pixel, the shader asks: "from the light, what's the nearest thing in this direction, and is my pixel farther than that?" If something nearer exists, my pixel is in its shadow.

The diagram shows the idea from the side. Each ray from the light stops at the first thing it hits. The pixel on the right of the occluder is farther from the light than the occluder is, so it is in shadow.

![A side view of a light, an occluder and the floor, with the shadowed stretch marked](/images/2026-09-28-shadows/shadow-map-idea.svg "The map stores the distance to the first thing each ray hits. A floor point behind the occluder fails the depth comparison and stays dark.")

That's a *depth comparison*, and it's cheap. The expensive part is making the maps, and making them look good.

### Spot lights and cube maps

A flashlight is a cone, so one map looking along the cone is enough. A wall lamp shines in all directions. For that I render a *cube shadow map*: six maps, one per face of a cube around the bulb (up, down, left, right, front, back). The shader picks the face by looking at which axis the direction from the lamp to the pixel points along most.

### One atlas for all of them

I didn't want dozens of separate textures to manage. Instead there is a single 4096 × 4096 depth texture cut into square tiles, an *atlas*. The flashlight takes one 1024 tile. Each shadowed lamp takes six 512 tiles. Tiles sit on a grid aligned to their own size, so finding room is a simple scan and nothing fragments. Each tile's view is widened by a small margin (a *guard band*) so that the soft filter never reads across into the neighbouring tile. Tests check all six cube faces, the seams between them and the corners.

## Choosing which lights get shadows

A hallway with a dozen lamps can't afford twelve cube maps. So the four lamps that matter most to the camera get one, ranked by brightness over squared distance. The flashlight always gets one.

Naive ranking has a nasty habit. Walk between two lamps of similar importance and the ranking flips back and forth, so shadows pop on and off. Two rules fix that.

- **Hysteresis.** A lamp keeps its place unless another lamp is at least 10% more important.
- **Fading.** When the choice does change, the new lamp's shadow fades in over 0.3 seconds while the old one fades out, so nothing pops.

A camera jump (a teleport, an editor focus, a benchmark pose) skips the fade and snaps straight to the right lamps. Otherwise a screenshot taken right after moving the camera caught shadows half faded in, which I found out the annoying way.

There are five lamp slots, not four. The fifth lets one lamp fade out while its replacement fades in, and 1 + 5 × 6 = 31 faces fits the 32-face buffer.

## Only redraw what changed

Drawing 25 tiles every frame would be wasteful, since a lamp on the wall doesn't move. So each tile remembers what it holds: its view, and every object inside that view. If nothing in it changed, it is not redrawn.

Measured through the editor's tool server:

- 25 tiles drawn on the first frame;
- 0 while nothing moves;
- 1 after the camera moves (the flashlight's tile);
- 5 after swinging the closet door, since only five cube faces can see it.

With caching switched off, redrawing all 25 tiles every frame costs 0.08 ms. In practice it is zero on a still pose.

## Soft, clean edges

A shadow map has a fixed resolution. Look closely and the edge is a staircase of its texels. Two techniques clean that up.

> **Concept: PCF filtering.** *Percentage-closer filtering* means that instead of one yes/no depth comparison, you do several nearby ones and average the answers. A pixel that is 40% "in shadow" gets 40% darkness, which turns a staircase into a soft edge. GPUs can do a small 2 × 2 version of this in hardware.

Every lookup in my shader takes 12 samples scattered in a small disc, and each one is itself a hardware-filtered 2 × 2 comparison. The disc is rotated differently at every pixel. That turns banding into fine noise, which the temporal anti-aliasing I built later averages away.

> **Concept: Shadow acne and bias.** A surface compared against its own depth map will sometimes decide it is shadowing itself. The depth stored in the map and the depth being tested are the same surface, but rounded slightly differently, so you get stripes or speckles called *acne*. The usual cure is a *bias*: nudge the comparison so the surface is a little closer to the light than it really is. Too little bias gives acne. Too much makes shadows detach from their objects ("peter-panning").

I wanted this to work without per-scene hand-tuned numbers. The surface is nudged along its normal and toward the light by amounts measured in *shadow texels at that distance*, so a far wall and a near wall are treated consistently. A slope term covers how much depth the filter spans on a tilted surface. That follows from the filter radius and the angle, so there is one fewer knob to tune. The old fixed rasteriser bias is gone, and the lamp walls stay clean.

## The flashlight sits off to the side

One more thing I learned: a torch held exactly at the eye would hide every shadow behind the object that casts it. If the light and the eye are in the same place, you only ever see the lit side. So the flashlight hangs off the camera, 26 cm to the right and 28 cm down. That small offset is what makes door frames throw a visible shadow band across a door.

## The look pass

With shadows coming from the right places, the old shadow strength of 0.72 let 28% of each light leak into its own shadow. I raised it to 0.92 and tightened the filter. Ambient light isn't shadowed, so shadows stay readable rather than going pitch black. I also lowered the flashlight a little.

![Door look before and after the tuning](/images/2026-09-28-shadows/door-look.jpg "Top: shadow strength 0.72, torch close to the eye. Bottom: strength 0.92, torch lower and to the right. The frames now shadow the doors.")

## What it cost

The shadow pass itself is free on still poses, because everything is cached. The cost shows up in the main scene pass: each pixel may now do up to five shadowed lights with 12 taps each.

| Pose | GPU frame before | after |
|---|---|---|
| south_start | 0.69 ms | 1.10 ms |
| north_corridor_long | 0.68 ms | 1.05 ms |
| north_locked_door | 0.61 ms | 0.99 ms |

That's about 0.35 ms more, in a frame that is still around 1 ms of a 6.5 ms budget. The bench is a Debug build on an RTX 4070 at 1440p.

## Still rough

The differences are deliberately quiet from still poses. The hallway has few objects between a lamp and a wall, so most of the image barely changes. It is the moving shadows while you walk that sell it. Two things bother me:

- The filter radius is fixed. Shadows should be sharp at contact and softer with distance (the effect called *contact hardening*), and mine aren't yet.
- Lamp selection doesn't know about walls, so a lamp in the next corridor can take a shadow slot even though its light is blocked. At four slots, with caching, it's harmless. If slots get scarce, I'll fix it.

I also found out my validation script had been hiding a failure since the previous day because it filtered the output and never checked the exit code. I fixed the script, and I'm telling you here because I wrote in my notes that the checks had passed, and they hadn't.
