After the lighting rewrite the hallway had one shadow. It came from a single projected map, aimed by hand from a spot near one lamp at a hand-picked target, and the flashlight cast no shadow at all. Light without shadow looks pasted onto the surface, and a horror scene depends on the dark shape behind a door frame. The goal for the day was that every shadow comes from a real light, from that light's position, with nothing aimed by hand.

## How a shadow map works

A shadow map answers one question for every lit pixel, whether anything lies between that point and the light. It does so in two passes.

1. **Render from the light.** The scene is drawn from the light's position into a depth texture, with no colour output. Each texel stores the distance to the nearest surface along its ray from the light. This texture is the shadow map.
2. **Compare in the camera pass.** When the camera pass shades a pixel, the shader transforms the pixel's world position into the light's clip space. That gives a texture coordinate and a depth `d`. It reads the stored depth `s` at that coordinate. If `d` is greater than `s`, something nearer to the light was recorded along that ray, and the pixel is in shadow.

![A side view of a light, an occluder and the floor, with the shadowed stretch marked](/images/2026-09-28-shadows/shadow-map-idea.svg "The map stores the depth of the first surface along each ray from the light. A floor point behind the occluder is farther away than that depth and stays dark.")

The comparison costs one texture read per light per pixel. The expense is in the first pass, which redraws geometry from every shadowed light, and in the quality problems described next.

### Resolution, acne and peter-panning

A shadow map has a fixed number of texels, and each texel covers a patch of the world whose size grows with distance from the light. For a square tile of `N` texels and a field of view `fov`, one texel at depth `z` is about `2 · tan(fov/2) · z / N` wide. For a 512 tile covering 90°, that is about 1.2 cm at 3 m and 3.1 cm at 8 m. Close to the lamp the edges are fine. Farther away each shadow edge is visibly a staircase of texels.

The second problem is *shadow acne*. The map stores the depth at the centre of each texel, while the pixel being shaded sits somewhere else inside that texel. On a surface that faces the light head-on the two depths agree. On a tilted surface the depth changes across a texel by roughly `texel size × tan θ`, where `θ` is the angle between the surface normal and the light direction. Some of the pixels end up slightly behind the stored depth and decide that the surface is shadowing itself. The result is stripes or speckles.

The usual fix is a *bias*: compare against a depth that is slightly closer to the light. Too little bias leaves acne. Too much makes shadows detach from the objects that cast them, which is called *peter-panning*, and lets light leak at contact points. A *normal offset* is a second tool. It moves the receiving point along its surface normal before it is projected, which handles grazing angles without a large depth bias.

### My bias, in shadow texels

I wanted a setup that needs no per-scene hand tuning, so both biases are expressed in shadow texels at the receiver's depth. The shader computes the world size of one texel there and scales everything by it:

```
texelWorld   = (2 · tan(fov/2) / tileSize) · depthFromLight
normalOffset = normalOffsetTexels · texelWorld · sin θ
depthBias    = texelWorld · (depthBiasTexels + (1 + filterRadius) · tan θ)
```

The normal offset grows toward grazing angles, where a texel spans the most depth. The `tan θ` term is the slope bias. It covers the depth that the filter footprint spans on a tilted surface, so it follows from the filter radius and the angle and is not a separate setting. The defaults are 1.5 texels of normal offset and 1 texel of constant bias. A far wall and a near wall are treated consistently, and the same code serves the flashlight and the lamp cubes. The old fixed rasteriser bias is gone, along with the earlier workaround that faded shadows out at grazing angles. The lamp walls stay clean.

The shadow pass also draws back faces, as I described in the previous post. The lamps sit on the very walls they light, and a wall's back face lies 8 cm behind its lit face, so the geometry's own thickness acts as extra bias at no cost.

## Spot lights, cube maps and the atlas

A spot light needs one map: a perspective frustum matching the light's cone. A wall lamp shines in all directions, so it gets a *cube shadow map*, six 90° frusta, one per axis direction. The shader chooses the face from the major axis of the vector from the light to the pixel, with ties going to X and then Y.

Both kinds share one texture. I did not want dozens of separate shadow textures to manage, so there is a single 4096 × 4096 depth texture, an *atlas*, cut into square tiles. At 32 bits per texel it takes 64 MB.

- The flashlight takes one 1024 tile. Each shadowed lamp takes six 512 tiles, one per cube face.
- Tiles are power-of-two squares on a grid aligned to their own size, with 256 as the smallest. Finding room is a scan for a free aligned block, and freeing never fragments the atlas.
- The flashlight and five lamp slots use about half of the atlas.

A filtered lookup near the edge of a tile would read texels that belong to the neighbouring tile. To prevent that, each tile's frustum is widened slightly, a *guard band*, so the cone or cube face lands a few texels inside the tile edge. The border is the filter radius rounded up plus two texels. Tests check all six cube faces, the seams between them and the corners: 420 directions, including edges and corners, stay inside the guard band.

## Choosing which lights cast shadows

A hallway with a dozen lamps cannot afford twelve cube maps. Each shadowed lamp costs up to six extra depth renders, and every pixel it reaches pays for the filtered comparison. So the four lamps that matter most to the camera get a cube map. The score is luminance times intensity divided by squared distance, with the bulb radius as a lower limit on the distance. The flashlight always gets a shadow.

A plain ranking has a flaw. Walk between two lamps of similar score and their scores cross repeatedly, so the shadowed set changes back and forth and each change makes a whole shadow appear or vanish. Two rules prevent that.

- **Hysteresis.** A slot keeps its lamp unless another lamp's score is at least 10% higher. The newcomer has to win by a margin.
- **Fading.** When the set does change, the new lamp's shadow fades in over 0.3 s while the old one fades out.

A camera jump, such as a teleport, an editor focus or a benchmark pose, skips the fade and selects the right lamps at once. Without that, a screenshot taken right after `camera_set` caught shadows half faded in. There are five lamp slots, not four, so that one lamp can fade out while its replacement fades in. With the flashlight, 1 + 5 × 6 = 31 faces, which fits the 32-face buffer.

## Redrawing only what changed

Drawing 25 tiles every frame would be wasteful, since a wall lamp does not move. Each tile remembers a hash of its view and of every object inside that view, computed from the exact float bits, so any movement changes it. A tile whose hash is unchanged is not redrawn. I measured the effect through the MCP `render_stats` tool:

- 25 tiles on the first frame: the flashlight plus four lamps times six faces;
- 0 while nothing moves;
- 1 after the camera moves, which is the flashlight's tile;
- 5 after swinging the closet door, because only five cube faces can see it.

With caching switched off, redrawing all 25 tiles costs 0.08 ms. On a still pose the cost is zero.

## Soft, filtered edges

The depth comparison is a yes or no per texel, which is why edges look like staircases. *Percentage-closer filtering* (PCF) averages several comparisons from nearby texels. A pixel for which 40% of the nearby comparisons say "in shadow" is darkened by 40%, and the staircase becomes a soft edge. The averaging has to be done on the comparison results, not on the stored depths, because averaging depths first would give a wrong depth.

My lookup has two levels. The shadow texture uses a comparison sampler, so each tap is a hardware 2 × 2 bilinear PCF. The shader then takes 12 taps in a Poisson disc, a fixed set of well-spread points, scaled by the filter radius in texels, which is 2 in the demo scene. The disc is rotated by a different angle at each pixel, using interleaved gradient noise whose phase advances every frame. That replaces banding with fine noise, and the temporal anti-aliasing I built later in the day averages the noise away. The final value is `lerp(1 − strength, 1, visibility)`, so a shadow removes a fixed share of that light and the ambient term is never shadowed.

## The flashlight sits off the eye

A torch held exactly at the eye would hide every shadow it casts behind the object casting it, because the eye and the light see the same side. So the flashlight hangs off the camera, 26 cm to the right and 28 cm down (`flashlight.offset`). That offset is what lets a door frame throw a visible shadow band across a door.

## Look pass

With shadows coming from the right places, the old shadow strength of 0.72 let 28% of each light leak into its own shadow. I raised it to 0.92 and tightened the filter. Ambient light is not shadowed, so shadows stay readable and do not go black. I also lowered the flashlight.

![Door look before and after the tuning](/images/2026-09-28-shadows/door-look.jpg "Top: shadow strength 0.72 and the torch close to the eye. Bottom: strength 0.92 and the torch lower and to the right. The frames now shadow the doors.")

The numbers are from a Debug build on an RTX 4070 at 1440p. The shadow pass is 0 ms on still poses because every tile is cached. The extra cost is in the scene pass, where up to five shadowed lights per pixel, each with 12 filtered taps, add about 0.35 ms. The frame is still about 1.1 ms of a 6.5 ms budget. Commits that only changed settings were pixel-identical.

The difference is quiet in still images. The hallway has few objects between a lamp and a wall, so most of the frame is unchanged. The moving shadows while walking are what show the work.

## Open issues

- The filter radius is constant, so every penumbra has the same width. A real shadow is sharp at the contact point and softer farther away. Contact hardening is not implemented.
- Lamp selection ignores walls. A lamp in the next corridor can take a shadow slot even though a wall blocks its light. With four slots and caching it does no harm, and I will fix it if slots become scarce.
- The painted door frames show stretched texture streaks on their side faces, because the world-projected UVs on thin boxes stretch. That is a material problem and predates the shadows.

The validation script also had a fault. It had reported success since the previous day while failing, because it filtered the output and never checked the exit code. My earlier notes said the checks had passed, and they had not. The script now tests the exit code.
