The lighting was physically based and the shadows were real, but two things that make an interior read as real were still missing. The first was the soft darkness where a wall meets the floor, the ceiling or a piece of trim. The second was a glossy floor that mirrors the lamps and door frames, instead of only carrying a highlight from the nearest one.

Both effects depend on the neighbourhood of a pixel: what is near it, and what is in the direction it reflects. The renderer already holds a depth buffer of everything visible, so both can be estimated from it in screen space, without a second render of the scene. Before I could do that, the renderer needed to record more about the scene than depth.

## Data from the depth prepass

The depth prepass runs first, so I made it write two more targets. One holds the world-space normal of each pixel, packed into two 16-bit values with octahedral encoding. The other holds roughness and metalness in two 8-bit channels. GTAO needs the normal and SSR needs the normal and roughness before the lighting pass has run.

> **Background: Octahedral normal encoding.** A unit normal has three components but only two degrees of freedom. Octahedral encoding projects the unit sphere onto an octahedron, folds the lower half onto the square, and stores the two coordinates. It decodes with a few operations and distributes precision evenly. A test checks that every direction round-trips within 0.004°.

The prepass now shades pixels instead of only writing depth, which costs about 0.17 ms.

## The Hi-Z depth pyramid

Both effects need to find geometry along a line across the screen. Walking along it pixel by pixel is expensive. A *hierarchical depth pyramid* (Hi-Z) makes it cheap. A compute pass copies the depth buffer as level 0. Each further level is half the size, and each texel keeps the nearest depth of the four texels below it. Odd sizes take three texels on the edge. A test confirms that every texel equals the minimum of the pixels it covers.

A coarse texel therefore answers a conservative question, whether anything in this block of pixels is closer than a given depth. If not, a ray crossing the block cannot hit anything in it, and it can skip the whole block in one step. The pyramid costs 0.07 ms.

## Ambient occlusion

In a real room light reaches a point from the lamps and also bounced from every other surface. The renderer does not yet compute bounced light, so it uses a flat ambient term that arrives equally from all directions. A point at the bottom of a corner can see only a small part of the room, because the walls block the rest, so it should receive less. *Ambient occlusion* (AO) estimates, per pixel, what fraction of the surrounding hemisphere is open, weighted by the cosine of the angle from the normal. It is why creases, the line under a baseboard and the edge of a door frame look anchored.

### How GTAO measures it

I use ground-truth ambient occlusion (GTAO, Jimenez et al. 2016), a horizon-based method. For each pixel, the shader works in a few *slices*, planes that contain the view vector and run across the screen in different directions. In each slice it marches along the depth buffer on both sides of the pixel and records the *horizon*, the highest elevation angle at which a nearby surface blocks the view. The open part of the slice lies between the two horizons.

![One slice through the depth buffer: the horizon on each side and the open sector between them](/images/2026-09-28-gtao-ssr/horizon.svg "Each horizon is the highest surface seen from the point on that side. The ambient light arrives through the open sector between h1 and h2.")

The cosine-weighted visible area of the slice has a closed form, which is why the method is called ground truth. With `n` the angle of the surface normal projected into the slice, and `h1` and `h2` the two horizon angles, all measured from the view vector:

```
visible = ¼ (−cos(2·h1 − n) + cos n + 2·h1·sin n)
        + ¼ (−cos(2·h2 − n) + cos n + 2·h2·sin n)
```

The shader multiplies this by the length of the projected normal and averages over the slices. I compared the formula with numerical integration, and the worst error was 2e-7.

### My implementation

- It runs at half resolution. Each texel is evaluated at full-resolution pixel `2i`, and the scene pass upsamples with weights that depend on depth difference, so a pixel never borrows occlusion from a different surface.
- There are 2 slices per pixel and 6 steps on each side, with a search radius of 0.55 m. The steps are spaced quadratically, `t² · radius`, so most samples fall near the pixel where detail matters.
- A sample farther than the radius should not darken the pixel, or a distant wall seen past a near edge would produce a dark halo. Each sample's weight is 1 within 40% of the radius and fades to zero at the radius.
- Far samples read coarser Hi-Z levels, because distant taps do not need full detail, and the min-depth levels lean toward occluders. Depth is reconstructed from a formula instead of a matrix multiply.
- The noise is a 4 × 4 interleaved pattern for the slice angle, plus a per-pixel offset for the step positions. A 5 × 5 depth-aware blur with half weight on its outer ring gives every residue of the 4 × 4 pattern equal total weight, so the pattern cancels exactly and the kernel stays centred.

The first version cost 0.6 to 0.7 ms at 1440p. Reading the distant samples from smaller pyramid levels and replacing the matrix multiply brought it to 0.18 ms with the same look.

### How the occlusion is applied

- **Ambient light** is multiplied by the full occlusion. Ambient is the light AO describes.
- **Direct light** receives 30% of the occlusion, blended as `lerp(1, visibility, 0.3)`. A lamp lights a corner nearly as brightly as an open wall, and the shadow maps already handle large occluders. The 30% stands in for small contact shadows that the maps cannot resolve.
- **Multi-bounce.** Light that bounces inside a crease brightens it again, and bright surfaces bounce more, so plain AO makes light surfaces too dark. Jimenez's cubic fit in the surface's albedo adds the light back.
- **Specular occlusion.** Reflections of ambient light are darkened with Lagarde's formula, which uses the AO value, the roughness and the view angle. It avoids storing a bent normal per pixel.

I also set the strength to 1.4 as an exponent on the measured visibility.

## Reflections on the floor

A glossy surface shows what lies in the mirror direction `r = reflect(−v, n)`. Real-time engines approximate this in several ways: a prefiltered cube map, which has no parallax and does not show nearby objects; a second render from a mirrored camera, which is expensive and limited to flat surfaces; hardware ray tracing; or screen-space reflections (SSR). SSR casts the ray against the depth buffer I already have and, where it hits, copies the colour that is already on screen.

### Tracing through the pyramid

A ray can be marched with fixed steps, but small steps are slow and large steps skip thin objects. The Hi-Z pyramid removes that trade-off. The ray is projected to screen space as a segment with an `x`, `y` and depth, and the trace works on a level `L` of the pyramid whose cells are `2^L` pixels wide:

1. Read the nearest depth stored in the cell that holds the ray's current position.
2. If the ray is in front of that nearest depth, nothing in the cell can block it yet. If it leaves the cell before reaching that depth, the ray jumps to the cell edge and moves up a level to take bigger steps.
3. If the ray reaches that depth inside the cell, it stops there and drops a level to look closer. If it is already behind the cell's nearest depth, it also drops a level.
4. At level 0, a pixel counts as a hit only when the ray is no more than a thickness behind the stored surface, 0.15 m in view space. Otherwise the ray is assumed to have passed behind a thin object and continues.

The cost grows roughly with the logarithm of the ray's length in pixels, not with the length. I cap it at 80 iterations and 8 m of ray, and the ray starts 1.5 pixels along to avoid hitting its own surface.

### Resolve, confidence and compositing

A half-resolution pixel on a surface rougher than 0.5 is skipped. For a smoother one the pass writes the hit position, the distance travelled and a confidence value. After the scene is lit, a resolve pass fetches the lit colour at the hit and blurs it by the width of the surface's glossy lobe, `roughness² · distance`, converted to pixels at the hit and limited to 48. A rougher surface, or a farther hit, scatters the reflection over a wider area.

Confidence fades toward zero in four situations:

- The hit is within 10% of the screen edge, where the colour behind it is unknown.
- The surface is close to the roughness cut-off.
- The hit is in the last quarter of the ray.
- The hit surface faces away from the ray.

The renderer already had a flat ambient reflection. If SSR simply added its colour, reflections would be counted twice. So the composite takes the ambient specular away in proportion to the confidence and adds the traced colour, weighted by the same confidence and by the split-sum reflectance. The addition happens before bloom, so a reflected lamp still glows.

I wanted to know the trace was right and not only plausible. A synthetic corridor with a floor, walls, a pillar and a thin board was ray-cast into a depth buffer. I traced 538 floor reflections through the pyramid and again with a slow fine march. All 538 agreed within one pixel, the rays passed behind the thin board, and none hit the iteration cap. My first reference march was too coarse near the vanishing point, where one pixel spans metres of depth. The pyramid trace was the correct one.

## Limits

Both effects are screen-space, so they know only what the camera currently sees.

- SSR cannot show anything off screen. Looking down at the floor, the reflection of the ceiling is missing, and so is anything behind the camera. Probes or another fallback would fill that.
- The depth buffer keeps only the nearest surface at each pixel, so it has no thickness. A thin object hides what is behind it from both effects, and the 0.15 m thickness limit is a compromise between missing thin objects and hitting walls the ray should pass behind.
- Alpha-tested surfaces are not in the prepass, so they cast no AO and receive no reflections. The demo has none.
- Surfaces rougher than 0.5, such as plaster, get no screen-space reflection. They keep the ambient specular only.
- The SSR ray is not randomised yet. The glossy blur comes from the resolve pass, because random ray directions would show as noise without a temporal filter.

## Tuning and results

I reduced the floor's normal-map strength from 0.78 to 0.55, so the lamp reflections read as a streak along the boards and not as scattered sparkles. I tried rougher floor boards and rejected them, because they blurred the reflections away. In this dark scene the change is subtle. Auto-exposure re-meters, and the ambient light that AO acts on is small. The visible gains are a darker crease along a baseboard and a streak down the floor that continues toward the far lamps.

| Pose | GPU frame before | after | GTAO | SSR | Hi-Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| south_start | 0.99 ms | 1.72 ms | 0.20 | 0.15 | 0.07 |
| east_floor_gloss | 0.90 ms | 1.71 ms | 0.18 | 0.25 | 0.07 |
| north_locked_door | 0.88 ms | 1.50 ms | 0.18 | 0.11 | 0.07 |

These are Debug-build numbers on an RTX 4070 at 1440p. The rest of the increase is the prepass shading pixels, about 0.17 ms, and the extra reads in the lighting pass. Scaling by the ratio of the two cards' throughput, I expect roughly 0.35 ms for AO and 0.45 ms for SSR on an RTX 3060, inside their budgets of 0.7 ms and 1 ms. That is an estimate and not a measurement.

I also fixed a bug from the camera work. Debug views such as albedo or normals were being metered by auto-exposure, so the camera stayed badly exposed for a few seconds after I switched back to the normal image. Data views now leave the exposure alone.

The reflection rays still use a fixed pattern. Making them scatter across the floor's gloss needs noise that changes every frame and a filter that averages it away. That is temporal anti-aliasing, which is the next step.
