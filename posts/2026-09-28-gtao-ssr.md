The lighting was physically based and the shadows were real, but two things that make a real interior look real were still missing. First, the soft darkness where a wall meets the floor, the ceiling or a piece of trim. Second, a glossy floor that actually mirrors the lamps and door frames instead of just carrying a highlight.

Both of those are really questions about the *neighbourhood* of a pixel: what is near it, and what is it looking at? Rendering engines have good tricks for answering them using only what's already on the screen. So before I could do either, the renderer needed to learn the shape of the scene before lighting it.

## Teaching the renderer the shape of the scene

The depth prepass I'd added to save time already runs first. I made it do two extra things. For every pixel it now records which way the surface faces (the *normal*, packed into two numbers using a trick called octahedral encoding that I verified round-trips to within 0.004 degrees), plus how rough and how metallic the surface is. Both effects need that data before the lighting pass starts.

### A depth pyramid

> **Concept: The Hi-Z pyramid.** Take the depth buffer. Make a half-size copy where every pixel remembers the *nearest* depth among the four it replaces. Do that again, and again, until you're down to a few pixels. This stack of ever-smaller maps is a *Hi-Z* (hierarchical depth) pyramid. When a ray needs to cross a big empty region of the screen, it can check one coarse texel to confirm "nothing here is closer than my ray" and leap over the whole region at once, instead of creeping pixel by pixel.

A short compute pass builds it. A test confirms that every texel is exactly the minimum of the pixels it covers, including for odd sizes.

## Ambient occlusion with GTAO

> **Concept: Ambient occlusion.** In a real room, light comes from everywhere: lamps, but also bounced light from walls and ceiling. A pixel at the bottom of a corner can only see a small slice of that surrounding room, because the walls block the rest. So it gets less bounced light and looks darker. *Ambient occlusion* (AO) estimates, per pixel, how much of the surrounding hemisphere is open. It's the reason corners, creases and the line under a baseboard look grounded.

I use **GTAO**, ground-truth ambient occlusion. For each pixel, the shader looks outward across the screen in a couple of directions and finds the *horizon*: the highest angle at which some nearby surface blocks the view. The lower the open sky of that little hemisphere, the more occluded the pixel. GTAO's formula for turning horizons into an occlusion value comes from integrating the real geometry, which is why it's called "ground truth" and gives better results than older, more hacky AO. I tested the slice formula against numerical integration, and the worst error was 2e-7.

How it's used matters as much as how it's computed:

- It darkens ambient light fully, because ambient is the light AO is about.
- It darkens direct light a little, only 30%, because a lamp lights a corner nearly as brightly as a wall.
- Light bouncing inside a crease is added back, more on bright surfaces, so corners don't go unnaturally black.
- Reflections of ambient light are darkened too, with a formula that uses the AO value, the roughness and the view angle.

It runs at half resolution, with a fixed noise pattern that a small blur cancels exactly. The first version cost 0.6 to 0.7 ms at 1440p. Two changes brought it down: reading the distant horizon samples from the smaller pyramid levels (distant samples don't need full detail), and computing depth with a formula instead of a matrix multiply. Same look, **0.18 ms**.

## Reflections on the floor

> **Concept: Screen-space reflections.** To get a mirror reflection you could render the whole scene again from the mirror's side. That's expensive. *Screen-space reflections* (SSR) cheat. For each pixel on a shiny surface, bounce the view ray off the surface like a mirror, and walk that ray through the picture you've already rendered until it hits something. Whatever colour is on screen at that hit is the reflection. It costs a ray walk per pixel and needs no second render.

Each half-resolution floor pixel does exactly that, and the Hi-Z pyramid is what makes it affordable: the ray takes big steps through empty space and small steps only near a surface. After lighting, the colour at the hit is fetched and blurred by how rough the floor is and how far the ray travelled, because a rougher surface scatters its reflection. The result is added back *before* bloom, so a reflected lamp still glows.

One subtle point. The renderer already had a flat "ambient reflection" fallback. If SSR simply added on top of it, reflections would be counted twice. So the SSR result *replaces* the ambient reflection by exactly as much as the trace is confident about. Confidence fades near the screen edge, at the end of the ray and on rough surfaces.

I wanted to be sure the trace was right, not just plausible. So I built a synthetic corridor (floor, walls, a pillar, a thin board), cast 538 floor reflections through the pyramid, and traced them again with a slow, fine step-by-step march. All 538 agreed within one pixel, and rays correctly passed behind the thin board. My first reference march was actually the wrong one: near the vanishing point one pixel spans metres of depth, and my slow march was too coarse. The pyramid trace was right.

## The limits, honestly

Both techniques are *screen-space*: they only know about what's currently visible. That has consequences.

- SSR can't reflect anything off screen. Look down at the floor and the reflected ceiling isn't there. Anything behind the camera is missing too. Probes or a fallback would fill this, but that's later work.
- Both effects only see the front surface of whatever is nearest. A thin object hides what's behind it.
- Objects drawn with an alpha test aren't in the prepass, so they cast no AO and receive no reflections. The demo has none.
- Rough surfaces get no reflections at all, which is correct for plaster. Only the floor, metal sconces and anything below roughness 0.5 do.

## Tuning

I toned the floor's normal map down from a strength of 0.78 to 0.55, so the lamp reflections read as a streak along the boards instead of scattered sparkles. AO strength went to 1.4. I tried rougher floor boards and rejected them, since they blurred the reflections away.

In this very dark scene the whole change is deliberately subtle: auto-exposure re-meters, and the ambient light that AO acts on is small. The wins are in the details: a darker crease along a baseboard, a streak down the floor that matches the lamp.

## What it cost

| Pose | GPU frame before | after | GTAO | SSR | Hi-Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| south_start | 0.99 ms | 1.72 ms | 0.20 | 0.15 | 0.07 |
| east_floor_gloss | 0.90 ms | 1.71 ms | 0.18 | 0.25 | 0.07 |
| north_locked_door | 0.88 ms | 1.50 ms | 0.18 | 0.11 | 0.07 |

The rest of the increase is the prepass now shading pixels (about 0.17 ms) and the extra reads in the lighting pass. These are Debug-build numbers on an RTX 4070. On a 3060 I'd expect roughly 0.35 ms for AO and 0.45 ms for SSR, comfortably inside my plan.

A bug from the previous day also turned up here: debug views like albedo or normals were being metered by auto-exposure, so the camera stayed badly exposed for a few seconds after switching back to the normal image. Data views now leave the exposure alone.

The reflections currently use a fixed ray pattern. Making the rays truly scatter across the floor's gloss needs a noise that changes every frame, and a way to average that noise away. That's what comes next, and it has a name: temporal anti-aliasing.
